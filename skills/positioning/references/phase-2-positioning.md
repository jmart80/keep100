# Phase 2 Reference: Positioning Frameworks

Dunford supplies the logic, Fletch supplies the homepage-ready translation, Raskin supplies the sales-deck narrative. Run them in that order and only as far as the asset requires.

## Dunford's 5-component process (Obviously Awesome, Sales Pitch)

Positioning is the deliberate act of defining how the product is the leader at delivering something a defined set of customers cares a lot about. It is not a tagline. The five components must be worked in strict sequence because each is the input to the next.

| # | Component | Key question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Competitive alternatives | What do I have to beat to win a deal? |
| 2 | Unique attributes | What do we have that alternatives do not? |
| 3 | Value themes | So what, for the customer? |
| 4 | Target segment | Who gets the most from this value? |
| 5 | Market category | What market do we intend to win? |
| +1 | Relevant trend (optional) | Why does this matter today? |

**Prep decisions before starting:** (a) scope: positioning the product or the company? (b) state: early-stage keeps positioning loose until patterns emerge in who loves the product and why; mature businesses tighten.

**Process:**

1. **List best-fit customers.** The ones who get it, buy fast, love it, refer, and resist churn. If no happy customers exist, stop; do not tighten positioning, the data does not exist. (This is the cold start branch.)
2. **Assemble a cross-functional team** when the user has one: sales knows what buyers compare against, CS knows why they stay, product knows what is hard to build, the CEO owns the narrative. Solo founders skip the workshop but still answer from each seat.
3. **Release positioning baggage.** Explicitly abandon the default position inherited from the product's history. Without this step, the old frame gets defended reflexively.
4. **List true competitive alternatives.** Ask what best customers would do if the product did not exist: direct competitors, indirect tools (spreadsheets, internal builds), and status quo. Roughly 40% of B2B deals are lost to "no decision," which means losing to pen and paper. Expect 2 to 5 groups. Differentiation only exists relative to these alternatives.
5. **Isolate unique attributes.** Capabilities the alternatives lack. Stay at feature and capability level here; benefits come next. Include technical moats, proprietary data, integrations, workflow advantages.
6. **Map attributes to value themes.** For each attribute ask "so what for the customer," then cluster. Expect 1 to 4 themes. Each theme must be demonstrably true with proof: stats, third-party validation, customer evidence.
7. **Determine who cares most.** Find the characteristics of customers who care intensely: title, vertical, size, workflow, maturity, trigger event. Almost comically narrow is correct at this stage.
8. **Choose the market frame.** Three strategies: head to head (win an existing market, requires superior differentiation), big fish small pond (dominate a subsegment, best for startups), create a new game (highest risk, requires double the education spend). The category sets buyer assumptions about competitors, features, and pricing. Categories emerge; companies rarely create them.
9. **Layer a trend only if it genuinely accelerates urgency** for this specific value. A forced trend backfires.
10. **Capture on a one-page canvas.** Positioning is the input to messaging, not the copy itself. The first translation output is a sales pitch structure, not a homepage.

**Sales pitch translation (Sales Pitch, 2023):** Setup (market insight, alternatives, "the perfect world") then Follow-Through (value themes with proof). The insight in the setup is what a buyer must understand about the market before the differentiated value makes sense. This structure informs homepage narrative order.

**Failure modes to check against:** default positioning (letting customers pick the category), investor pitch creep (marketing the roadmap instead of current value), mistaking a marketing problem for a positioning problem, status quo blindness, differentiating too late in the pitch, and startups positioning on the roadmap instead of what ships today.

## Fletch's minimum viable positioning (Pierri and Kaminski, Fletch PMM)

**Core equation:** Positioning = product differentiation + customer segmentation.

**The anchor decision** (make this explicitly and record it):

- **Category-based:** anchor in a well-understood category plus a differentiator. Example: Figma as collaborative interface design tool.
- **Use-case-based:** anchor in a specific, well-understood workflow. Example: Calendly as scheduling without back-and-forth email.

Neither is better in general. Choose whichever makes the value easier to explain to this audience. Dunford works inside-out (what we uniquely have, then what category to claim); Fletch works outside-in (which anchor is clearest to the buyer). Run Dunford first for the foundation, then use Fletch to translate.

**The 7-box canvas** (single source of truth for every homepage section, value prop, and sales call):

1. Company type
2. Target department or persona
3. Product category
4. Primary use case
5. Competitive alternative
6. Problem with the current approach
7. Differentiation

**Product-led versus audience-led positioning:**

- **Product-led:** the capability itself is the differentiator. Lead with what the product does. Use when the capability is understood and solves a concrete workflow. Pierri: "Don't lead with the outcome, end with the outcome. Lead with what the product actually does; that's more differentiated." This deliberately contradicts generic lead-with-benefits advice.
- **Audience-led:** the audience's pain or context is the clearer hook. Lead with who it is for and the problem. Use for horizontal products or capabilities that need explanation.

**The 8-component messaging model** (used for auditing in Phase 5): market elements are persona, company type, context, problem; product elements are product category, capability, feature, benefit. Value props connect use case, current tool, problem, capability, feature, benefit in that chain.

## Raskin's strategic narrative (The Greatest Sales Deck)

A strategic narrative is the story in the buyer's head that guides their actions, larger than the company and valid independent of the vendor. Positioning answers where you compete and why you win; narrative answers why the world is changing and why buyers must act now.

**Five steps:**

1. Name a big, relevant change in the world. External, undeniable, not a product feature.
2. Show there will be winners and losers. Stakes must be real and specific to the buyer.
3. Tease the promised land: the desirable end state, not yet mentioning the product, not achievable alone.
4. Introduce capabilities as magic gifts the hero (the buyer) needs to reach the promised land.
5. Present evidence. Proof only works after the story is believed.

**Routing rule (resolves the apparent conflict with Laja):** Raskin's framework is for sales decks and fundraising narratives. Laja's message testing data governs websites, where nobody cares about the why before the what is crystal clear. These do not conflict once separated by asset. Never open a homepage with the world-shift.

**Prerequisite:** the Dunford canvas. A narrative built before positioning produces inspiring stories that do not convert; the buyer is moved but does not know what to do next. Narrative work is CEO work; a narrative marketing writes alone will not drive strategy.

**When to route to Raskin:** losing deals because buyers do not understand why the market is changing; raising a round; aligning an enterprise sales team on one story.
