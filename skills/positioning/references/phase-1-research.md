# Phase 1 Reference: Research Protocols

Three protocols, run together. JTBD supplies the switching story, message mining supplies the language at volume, the trigger technique supplies the campaign-ready moment. All three feed the same verbatim corpus.

## JTBD interviews (Bob Moesta, Demand-Side Sales 101)

**Who:** 5 to 10 people who recently switched, including recent churns. Recency matters; memories of the struggle decay fast.

**The Four Forces.** A customer switches only when push plus pull outweighs anxiety plus habit:

```
[Push of the situation] + [Pull of the new solution]
    >
[Anxiety about the new] + [Habit / inertia of the old]
```

Most teams amplify push and pull. The faster wins usually come from reducing anxiety and habit, the forces competitors ignore. Anxiety and habit patterns map directly to objection-handling sections and FAQ copy.

**Interview protocol:**

1. Set the stage: "I just want to hear your story." Conversational, not interrogative. Match tone and energy.
2. Establish context: role, company, what normal looked like before the switch.
3. Find the first thought: "What was happening the day you decided to search for a new tool?" Get to the exact moment.
4. Map the Four Forces for the switch: the push that made them look, the pull of the new approach, the anxiety that nearly stopped them, the habit that kept the old way comfortable.
5. Extract hiring and firing criteria: "What made you say, this is the one?" and "What nearly stopped you?"
6. Timeline the journey: before, during, and after the decision.

**Rules:** Ask what happened, never why they bought. Reject vague answers; if they say "it was too complicated," ask "what do you mean by that?" People buy to solve problems, not to get features.

**Synthesis across interviews:** aggregate the forces, find recurring struggles across personas (these become the pain vocabulary), compare personas for divergent forces, and separate edge cases from core insights. The struggling-moment language is the single most valuable input for hero copy: it is what buyers said before they knew the product existed, which is exactly the language the hero section should mirror.

**Artifacts:** one Four Forces map per interview; a cross-interview pattern table; a hiring and firing criteria list.

## Message mining (Joanna Wiebe, Copyhackers)

**Principle:** "Instead of writing your message on a blank page, steal it. Steal it directly from the mouths of your prospects." Prospects articulate value better than copywriters because they lived the problem and paid for the solution. The job is to listen, organize, and mirror back.

**Sources, in priority order:**

1. G2, Capterra, TrustRadius, Amazon, and app store reviews of the product and close competitors. Three-star reviews are the richest; they are specific and emotionally honest.
2. Sales call recordings, transcribed and coded.
3. Support tickets; recurring questions reveal what the product fails to explain.
4. Community posts: Reddit, Slack communities, LinkedIn comments, niche forums where the ICP spends time.
5. Confirmation-page surveys, which capture motivation at the moment of purchase.
6. On-site surveys and user tests of non-converters.
7. Customer interviews, transcribed rather than summarized.

**Process:**

1. Define the optimization target: which page, which conversion goal, which question the copy must answer.
2. Collect 50 to 200+ raw verbatims into one corpus. Copy and paste exactly. Summarizing destroys the data.
3. Tag each verbatim: scenario or context, goal or job ("I want to..."), benefit, pain, anxiety or objection, analogy or metaphor. Metaphors are often the stickiest copy.
4. Cluster tags into before state (current pains) and dream state (desired future).
5. Count frequency and note intensity. Frequency sets the messaging hierarchy (what goes above the fold). Intensity flags the most resonant hooks.
6. Hunt unexpected phrasing. The phrases that surprise you are the ones that make buyers feel read-minded.
7. Map to page structure: most frequent and most intense pains to the hero, mid-tier pains to benefit sections, anxieties to objection handling and FAQs.

## Trigger technique (Katelyn Bourgoin, Customer Camp)

A trigger event is the moment a buyer first realizes the problem is worth solving. The journey runs trigger, research, shortlist, decision; intercepting at the trigger is the highest-intent moment available.

**Four questions per buyer story:**

1. **Trigger:** what event started the buying journey?
2. **Job:** what job are they hiring a product to do?
3. **Pains with alternatives:** what failed about what they tried or considered?
4. **Selfish desire:** what does the buyer personally want (career, status, safety)?

Sources: the JTBD interviews above, detailed reviews (reviewers often recount their trigger journey), and the survey question "When did you realize you needed a product like ours?"

The technique is deliberately lighter than full JTBD: a 10-minute interview covering the four questions is enough to design a trigger-intercept campaign (search ads on trigger language, LinkedIn targeting on role changes, outbound sequenced on job postings). Use it when speed matters or as a compression of the JTBD data already collected.
