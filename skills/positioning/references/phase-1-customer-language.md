# Phase 1: Customer language

Three methods feed one collection of quotes. Conversations supply the story of the switch, review mining supplies language at volume, and the trigger questions supply the moment to reach people.

## Switch conversations (Bob Moesta, Demand-Side Sales 101)

**Who:** five people who recently changed how they handle the problem, or tried to. Recent matters, because the memory of the struggle fades. `keep100:customer-conversations` finds them, writes the invitation and files the notes.

**The Four Forces.** A person switches only when the push of their situation plus the pull of the new way outweighs their anxiety about the new plus the habit of the old.

Most founders talk only about push and pull. Lowering anxiety and habit is often the easier win, and what you learn about them becomes the FAQ and the top objection.

**What to listen for:**

1. The first thought: what was happening the day they decided the old way wasn't working.
2. The push that made them look, and the pull of what they hoped for.
3. The anxiety that nearly stopped them, and the habit that kept the old way comfortable.
4. What made them say "this one", and what nearly made them walk away.

Ask what happened, never why they bought. When an answer is vague ("it was too complicated"), ask what they mean.

The words people use for the struggle, before they knew a product existed, are the most valuable input for a headline.

## Message mining (Joanna Wiebe, Copyhackers)

**Principle:** don't write the message on a blank page. Take it from the people who have the problem. They describe it better than a copywriter because they lived it.

**Sources a founder with no customers can reach, in order:**

1. Reviews of close competitors and substitutes. Middling reviews are the richest, because they are specific and honest.
2. Community posts where the ideal customer talks: forums, subreddits, groups.
3. Replies to the founder's own outreach, and anything a prospect wrote.
4. Notes and transcripts in `conversations/`.

**Process:**

1. Collect the raw quotes in one place. Copy exactly. Summarizing destroys the data. Follow the verbatim rule: a quote is `[verbatim]` only when the exact words were seen.
2. Tag each: situation, goal ("I want to..."), pain, hesitation, trigger, comparison. Comparisons ("it's like...") often make the stickiest copy.
3. Sort into the before state (current pains) and the after state (what they want).
4. Count how many people raised each thing, and note how strongly. How often sets what goes at the top of the page. How strongly marks the best hooks.
5. Look for phrasing that surprises you. Those are the lines that make a reader feel understood.

Twenty quotes is the floor for this skill. More is better, and volume matters less than coming from the right people.

## Trigger questions (Katelyn Bourgoin, Customer Camp)

A trigger is the moment a buyer first decides the problem is worth solving. For each story, answer four questions:

1. **Trigger:** what event started the search?
2. **Job:** what are they hiring a product to do?
3. **Pains with alternatives:** what failed about what they tried?
4. **Personal want:** what does the buyer want for themselves (time back, less worry, to look competent)?

Reviews often recount the trigger. So does the question "when did you realize you needed something like this?" The triggers found here go into `positioning.md` and become the reasons to write in `keep100:prospect-list`.
